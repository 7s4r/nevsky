01 Believing God in order to know him

We do not “prove” God, any more than we prove our own existence: we live it. We exist, we live, we ask questions — and this simple fact already turns us towards the One who is the source of life. Many people who call themselves atheists have above all rejected false images of God that were poorly presented to them; often, deep down, they keep an intuition of the true God.

Being a believer is not first of all believing in God the way one subscribes to an idea; it is believing God, that is, trusting Someone who has something to say to us. This requires knowing how to keep silence in order to hear him.

The Church is precisely the place where God speaks to man and where man speaks to God. She is like a cross: a vertical bar, the movement of our whole being towards God; a horizontal bar, our life in the world — work, family, society. The Church never sets one against the other, because God himself became man in Jesus Christ.

What the Apostles handed on

  • Who God is: the Trinity, one God in three Persons; the Son became incarnate of the Holy Spirit and the Virgin Mary, the Mother of God.
  • What God does: he frees us from the dead end in which humanity shut itself by turning away from him, and enables us to become children of the Father by grace — this is faith, these are the sacraments, beginning with baptism.
  • The Holy Spirit remains: he keeps the Church from error, constantly renews her and lets us drink from the divine source.
It is impossible to live without life. And the existence of life comes from participation in God. To participate in God is to know him and to enjoy his goodness.St Irenaeus of Lyons († 202)

02 The Holy Trinity: one God in three Persons

Man is created in the image of God. To know who we are, we must therefore know who God is. Each of us says “I” — this is the mystery of the unique — and no one can live alone: we also say “we” — this is the mystery of the many. Left to ourselves, we cannot reconcile the two: the individual rises up against the group, or the group crushes the individual.

By revealing himself as Trinity, God reveals man to himself. In God, the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit are not opposed: each one “lets the other be” in perfect love, and they are one. A person, in the image of God, is therefore not an individual closed in on himself: it is a unique and irreplaceable being who turns towards the other and says “you”. To live in this way is to commune — and this communion is the Church.

The FatherSource of the Godhead, without beginning, beyond time.
The SonThe Word, eternally begotten of the Father, of one nature with him.
The Holy SpiritProceeds eternally from the Father, worshipped and glorified with the Father and the Son.

The Father is the source (the “monarchy”) of the divine life, and yet the Son and the Spirit are fully equal to him: three Persons, one nature, one God. That is why the word Orthodoxy means both “right belief” and “right glory”: “Glory to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit, now and ever, and unto ages of ages.”

No sooner do I conceive of the One than I am illumined by the splendour of the Three; no sooner do I distinguish them than I am carried back to the One… When I bring the Three together in one thought, I see a single torch.St Gregory of Nazianzus (328-390)

03 How did God reveal himself as Trinity?

The Trinity is not an invention of philosophers: it is revealed in history, step by step.

  • At the oak of Mamre (Genesis 18): Abraham receives three visitors and speaks to them in the singular — “Lord, do not pass by your servant”. This is the scene depicted in St Andrei Rublev’s famous icon of the Trinity.
  • At the baptism of Christ in the Jordan (Mark 1:9-11): the Father makes his voice heard, the Spirit descends in the form of a dove upon the Son. This is the Theophany, the “manifestation of God”.
  • At the Transfiguration on Mount Tabor (Mark 9:2-8): the bright cloud signifies the Spirit, and the Father proclaims “This is my beloved Son: listen to him”.
  • In Christ’s teaching (Gospel of John): “The Father is in me and I in the Father” (10:38); “The Comforter, the Holy Spirit, whom the Father will send in my name, will teach you all things” (14:26).
When Thou, O Lord, wast baptized in the Jordan, the worship of the Trinity was made manifest: for the voice of the Father bore witness to Thee, calling Thee his beloved Son, and the Spirit in the form of a dove confirmed the truth of his word.Troparion of the Theophany

04 Unknowable and yet near

Our life itself is woven with mystery: love, suffering, the longing for a happiness we never quite reach. With each new awareness we stand before a beginning: life is a series of initiations. And at the heart of it all there is not “something” but Someone: God who acts.

God is not only “above” us: he is the Wholly Other. “I am who I am” (Exodus 3:14). Our intellect cannot confine him in formulas. That is why Orthodox theology is first of all apophatic: it approaches God by saying what he is not, stripping us of our images and ready-made ideas.

Essence and energies: the Orthodox key

  • In his essence (his inner nature), God remains forever inaccessible.
  • Yet he truly gives himself through his uncreated energies: his grace, his light, his life. It is not “something” of God, but God himself who communicates himself.
  • This is the light the Apostles beheld on Tabor, and that the saints have seen in prayer: “one must become light oneself to approach the One who is light”.
Faith is light, but faint; hope is light, but still imperfect; it is in the fullness of Love that light reaches its perfection.Archimandrite Sophrony (Sakharov), 1952

05 Christ: the Truth made man

The Son, God equal to the Father and the Spirit, became man. He took his place in our history; he was born, grew up and lived through every stage of our existence. What he brings is not merely one doctrine or wisdom among others: it is the fullness of Truth, a Truth that is no longer “something” but Someone. There is no longer an abyss between God and man.

Human wisdom has often swung between two extremes: a God so distant that he becomes inaccessible, or a humanism that ends up doing without God. The overwhelming newness of the Gospel is God and man: Christ brings both.

The words of Scripture are like birds: we can read a passage a thousand times without its touching us, and then one day it seizes us. On that day, the Holy Spirit has given us eyes to see. This is what Tradition is: the Holy Spirit himself becoming the memory and discernment of the Church.

06 Christ, the balance of our life

Two imbalances always threaten the spiritual life:

  • “Spirit only”: nothing but God, nothing but the spirit; the body would be contemptible, matter dirty, the world a prison. Such disembodied piety makes people harsh, intolerant, sometimes inhuman.
  • “Human only”: nothing but man and the earth, with God set aside as an optional comfort.

Before the tomb of Lazarus, Jesus weeps — and then says: “Lazarus, come out!” The man weeps and God raises the dead, in one and the same Person. In Christ, everything finds its rightful place: the divine does not crush the human, and the human does not drive out the divine. The body, work, tenderness, a good shared meal, the beauty of a garden all have value and dignity. Christ ate, drank, slept and loved his friends.

In him, the divine has entered the human so that man may share in the divine life and thus become fully himself.

07 From heresies to the dogma of Chalcedon

The Church proclaims a dogma when an error threatens people’s access to the life-giving truth. Dogmas are not straitjackets: they are markers along the way. “If you go this way, there is life; if you stray from it, you lose your way.”

In the first centuries the temptation was always the same: to refuse that God truly became man, and to adjust Revelation to our own ideas.

DocetismChrist’s humanity was supposedly only an appearance.→ St John, St Ignatius of Antioch and St Irenaeus defend Christ’s real flesh.
ArianismThe Son was supposedly only a creature, not truly God.→ Nicaea (325): “of one essence with the Father”.
ApollinarianismChrist supposedly had a human body and soul, but no human mind.→ Constantinople (381): Christ is wholly man.
NestorianismMary was supposedly only “mother of Christ”, not Mother of God.→ Ephesus (431): Mary is Theotokos.
MonophysitismChrist’s divinity supposedly absorbed his humanity.→ Chalcedon (451): two natures, one Person.

The first four Ecumenical Councils

  1. 325

    Nicaea I

    The Son is “true God of true God, of one essence with the Father”. First part of the Symbol of Faith.

  2. 381

    Constantinople I

    Divinity of the Holy Spirit, “who proceeds from the Father”. Final wording of the Nicene-Constantinopolitan Creed.

  3. 431

    Ephesus

    The Virgin Mary is proclaimed Theotokos, Mother of God.

  4. 451

    Chalcedon

    Christ, one Person in two natures, divine and human, “without confusion, without change, without division, without separation”.

The dogma of Chalcedon, explained

  • “Following the holy Fathers”: the people of God recognise themselves in the faith proclaimed; it is concord, not submission.
  • “We all teach with one voice to confess”: to confess is to commit oneself wholly to a truth that surpasses our understanding.
  • One and the same Son, “Son of God” and “Son of Man”: not two sons, but a twofold sonship.
  • Lord (the divine name), Jesus (the human name), Christ (the anointed, deified man): three names that sum up our vocation — to come from God, to be fully human, to be deified.
  • “Like us in all things except sin”: on the Cross he went through utter abandonment (“My God, why hast thou forsaken me?”), while remaining the Creator of the worlds.
In the grave bodily, in hades with thy soul as God, in paradise with the thief, and on the throne with the Father and the Spirit wast thou, O boundless Christ, filling all things.Troparion of the Paschal Hours

08 The Holy Spirit, creation and deification

God creates the world out of the overflowing of his love: in order to say “you” to someone else. He creates all things through his Word, in the Spirit, and each thing bears his signature: unity in diversity.

Man is created in the image of God in order to attain his likeness. The goal of all creation is theosis, deification: to become by grace what God is by nature. “God became man so that man might become god,” said St Athanasius of Alexandria.

Grace and freedom: synergy

  • Salvation is not an external verdict: it is a healing and a transfiguration of the whole person — body, soul and spirit.
  • God’s grace never forces itself on us; it waits for our free “yes”. Two movements come together: God’s and man’s. This is synergy.
  • The Holy Spirit is the one who works this transformation: he acts in every sacrament, and the Church calls upon him ceaselessly — “O Heavenly King, Comforter, Spirit of Truth… come and abide in us”.

Sin, at bottom, is the choice to become “like gods” without God. Christ, the new Adam, reopens the way: by his death he trampled down death, and by his resurrection he opens eternal life to us. Our very bodies will rise again, transfigured.

This world passes away so that grace may come. And grace comes. This world passes away; grace will not pass away.After the catechesis of Fr Georges Lusseaud
Section II · The Creed

The Nicene-Constantinopolitan Creed

Drawn up by the Councils of Nicaea (325) and Constantinople (381), the Symbol of Faith is sung by the whole congregation at every Divine Liturgy and proclaimed at baptism. Here it is in twelve articles, each with an explanation in today’s language.

Liturgical textI believe in one God, the Father Almighty, Maker of heaven and earth, and of all things visible and invisible.

“I believe”: faith is a personal act, trust given to God who speaks. We do not say “I think that”, but “I believe” — I commit myself.

“One God, the Father”: the Father is the source of the Godhead; from him the Son is begotten and the Spirit proceeds. God is not an anonymous force: he is Father, and we may call him “Our Father”.

“Heaven and earth, all things visible and invisible”: everything that exists comes from God — matter as well as the spiritual world of the angels. Nothing in creation is evil in itself; “heaven” also refers to the angelic world, and “earth” to the human world.

Go further: ·

Liturgical textAnd in one Lord Jesus Christ, the Son of God, the Only-begotten, begotten of the Father before all ages; Light of Light, true God of true God, begotten, not made, of one essence with the Father, by whom all things were made.

This article answers the heresy of Arius, for whom the Son was only a superior creature. The Council of Nicaea affirms on the contrary that the Son is begotten, not made: he has no beginning, he is eternally Son.

“Light of Light”: just as light shines from the sun without diminishing it, the Son is born of the Father without separation. “Of one essence” (Greek homoousios): of the same nature, the same being as the Father.

“By whom all things were made”: the Word is the Creator. The one who will be born in Bethlehem is the one through whom the universe exists.

Go further:

Liturgical textWho for us men and for our salvation came down from heaven, and was incarnate of the Holy Spirit and the Virgin Mary, and became man.

“For us and for our salvation”: the Incarnation is not a show; it is an act of love. God lowers himself — this is kenosis — to meet us where we are.

“Of the Holy Spirit and the Virgin Mary”: the Spirit is the author of this conception, and Mary freely consents to it on behalf of all humanity. Where Eve had said no, Mary says “Be it unto me according to thy word”.

“And became man”: not the appearance of a man, but a complete man — body, soul and spirit — while remaining fully God.

Go further: ·

Liturgical textAnd was crucified also for us under Pontius Pilate, and suffered, and was buried.

“Under Pontius Pilate”: the mention of a Roman governor anchors our faith in real history. Christianity is not a timeless myth.

“And suffered”: Christ truly suffered in his flesh and in his soul, even to abandonment. He lived our life and our death, so that none of our distress would be foreign to God.

The Cross is not a defeat: in the Orthodox tradition it is always radiant, the “life-giving Cross”, for it is the Lord of glory who is lifted up on it. From Christ’s pierced side flow blood and water: the Eucharist and baptism — that is, the Church.

Liturgical textAnd the third day he rose again, according to the Scriptures.

The heart of the whole Christian faith. Christ descended into Hades to shatter its gates: this is what the icon of the Resurrection shows, where he raises Adam and Eve from their tombs.

“According to the Scriptures”: the Resurrection fulfils the promises of the Old Testament. It is not a mere return to life, but the entry of our humanity into incorruptible divine life.

Christ is risen from the dead, trampling down death by death, and upon those in the tombs bestowing life.Paschal Troparion

Go further:

Liturgical textAnd ascended into heaven, and sits at the right hand of the Father.

At the Ascension Christ does not abandon his own: he carries our human nature into the heart of the Trinity. A man — our flesh — now sits “at the right hand of the Father”, that is, in divine glory.

Since then he remains among us invisibly: in the Church, in the Eucharist, in prayer. “I am with you always, even unto the end of the world” (Matthew 28:20).

Liturgical textAnd he shall come again with glory to judge the living and the dead, whose kingdom shall have no end.

Christ was, he is, he is coming. The first Christians prayed: Maranatha — “Come, Lord!” This return, the Parousia, will be the dazzling manifestation of what is already true: Christ is victorious.

Judgement is not first of all a threat: it is the light that reveals each thing as it is. The one who judges is the one who gave his life for us. His kingdom, the Eighth Day, will have no end.

Liturgical textAnd in the Holy Spirit, the Lord, the Giver of Life, who proceeds from the Father, who with the Father and the Son together is worshipped and glorified, who spoke by the prophets.

“The Lord”: the Spirit is not an impersonal force; he is a divine Person, God like the Father and the Son.

“Who proceeds from the Father”: the Orthodox Church keeps the text of the Creed as the Councils established it, without additions. The Father is the sole source of the Son and of the Spirit — which underlies the balance of the whole life of the Church.

“The Giver of Life”: it is the Spirit who acts in every sacrament. The whole life of the Church is an epiclesis, an invocation of the Spirit. He is also the one who “spoke by the prophets” and who opens our minds to the Scriptures.

Go further: ·

Liturgical textIn one Holy, Catholic and Apostolic Church.
  • One: a communion gathered around the same Eucharist, in the same faith.
  • Holy: not because her members are perfect, but because Christ sanctifies her and the Spirit dwells in her.
  • Catholic (from the Greek kath’holon, “according to the whole”): she possesses the fullness of the faith and embraces all creation.
  • Apostolic: founded on the Apostles, she keeps their witness alive through the succession of bishops and through Tradition.

Go further:

Liturgical textI acknowledge one baptism for the remission of sins.

Baptism is a new birth: immersed three times in water in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit, the newly baptised dies with Christ and rises with him. He “puts on Christ” (Galatians 3:27).

“One”: baptism is not repeated. In the Orthodox Church it is immediately followed by chrismation (the gift of the Spirit) and communion: this is complete Christian initiation, for little children too.

After baptism, it is confession that constantly renews baptismal grace; it is sometimes called a “second baptism”, the baptism of tears.

Liturgical textI look for the resurrection of the dead.

Christian faith does not merely hope for the survival of a soul: it awaits the resurrection of the body. Our body, fragile and mortal today, will be a “glorious body”, incorruptible, in the image of the risen Christ.

That is why the Church honours the body, prays for the departed “from Adam to this day”, and venerates the relics of the saints. “I look for”: the whole Christian life is stretched out towards this future.

Liturgical textAnd the life of the age to come. Amen.

The “age to come” is the Kingdom of God: the whole of creation transfigured, where “God shall be all in all” (1 Corinthians 15:28). It is not an extension of time, but the fullness of life with God.

This Kingdom is already tasted here below: the Divine Liturgy begins with “Blessed is the Kingdom of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit”. Icons are like windows onto it.

“Amen”: “yes, it is true, I commit myself to it”. The whole profession of faith is sealed with this word, which is already a prayer.

The complete Symbol of Faith

I believe in one God, the Father Almighty, Maker of heaven and earth, and of all things visible and invisible.

And in one Lord Jesus Christ, the Son of God, the Only-begotten, begotten of the Father before all ages; Light of Light, true God of true God, begotten, not made, of one essence with the Father, by whom all things were made;

who for us men and for our salvation came down from heaven, and was incarnate of the Holy Spirit and the Virgin Mary, and became man.

And was crucified also for us under Pontius Pilate, and suffered, and was buried. And the third day he rose again, according to the Scriptures; and ascended into heaven, and sits at the right hand of the Father; and he shall come again with glory to judge the living and the dead, whose kingdom shall have no end.

And in the Holy Spirit, the Lord, the Giver of Life, who proceeds from the Father, who with the Father and the Son together is worshipped and glorified, who spoke by the prophets.

In one Holy, Catholic and Apostolic Church. I acknowledge one baptism for the remission of sins. I look for the resurrection of the dead, and the life of the age to come. Amen.

Section III · Spiritual & liturgical life

Seven Keys to the Spiritual Life

Seven doors into the concrete life of the Church. Open each key for an in-depth reading.

Key 1

The Mother of God and the Saints

Mary, the Theotokos, “more honourable than the Cherubim”, and the great cloud of saints who go before us on the way.

Mary, the Mother of our Lord Jesus Christ, conceived by the Holy Spirit and not of human seed. The Church confesses her Ever-Virgin: a virgin before, during and after giving birth. And since the Council of Ephesus (431) she proclaims her Theotokos, Mother of God — for the one she brought into the world is not “a man in whom God dwelt”, but God himself made man.

Virginity: transparency to God

In its deepest sense, virginity is not merely abstinence; it is transparency to God, becoming “brand new” for him, free from habit and routine. Imagine a peaceful river reflecting the sky: stir up the mud and the water clouds over and reflects nothing. Let it settle, and the sky is mirrored in it again. For us sinners, purity of heart is less a state to preserve than one to win, with the help of grace.

The new Eve

Eve, free and transparent to God, said “no”; all humanity was drawn into her fall. Mary, at the end of generations of holiness prepared by God, freely says “yes” on behalf of all humanity: “Be it unto me according to thy word.” Her bodily virginity is the visible sign that the child born of her comes from God, and is God. To deny Mary’s virginity is, at bottom, to weaken faith in Christ, true God and true man.

More honourable than the Cherubim, and beyond compare more glorious than the Seraphim, without corruption thou gavest birth to God the Word: true Theotokos, we magnify thee.Hymn to the Mother of God, sung at every Liturgy

The communion of saints

We venerate the saints because they went before us and showed us the way. A saint, said Bishop Jean of Saint-Denis, is “one who feeds on God and breathes him”. Holiness is not a moral performance or an accumulation of merits: it is the deliberate and final choice of the love of God, a whole life attuned to the rhythm of the Trinity.

We do not “worship” them — worship is due to God alone — but we honour them and ask them to pray for us, as we would ask a friend to pray for us. With them, the Church on earth and the Church in heaven form one single family.

Key 2

The Angels and the Heavenly Powers

The invisible world is more real and more populated than the visible one: nine angelic choirs in the service of God and of mankind.

“Maker of heaven and earth”: in the Creed, heaven also refers to the angelic world, and earth to the human world. God’s work always leads to someone: in the visible world, to man; in the invisible world, to the angel.

The angel is not a “pure spirit” in the sense that God alone is: though bodiless in relation to us, he remains a limited creature before God. His greatness comes from being wholly at the service of God — never an “individual” turned in on himself, always a person turned towards the Other. The angel is like a mirror reflecting the divine will. He claims nothing for himself.

The names of the angels

Their names end in -el, meaning “God”: Michael, “Who is like God?”, leader of the heavenly hosts; Gabriel, “Strength of God”, messenger of the Annunciation; Raphael, “God heals”. The Church’s restraint regarding them (Council of Laodicea) reminds us that the veneration of angels must never obscure the worship of God.

The nine angelic choirs

According to the tradition handed down by St Dionysius the Areopagite, the angels are arranged in three triads — three times three, a reflection of the Trinity. Only the Mother of God is exalted above them.

Highest triad — closest to GodSeraphim · Cherubim · Thrones
The highest initiates of love, of knowledge and of obedience.
Middle triad — the order of the cosmosDominions · Virtues · Powers
They serve the will of God, the true “law of nature”.
Triad closest to manPrincipalities · Archangels · Angels
Protection of nations and cities, divine messengers, guardian angels.
Jacob dreamed, and behold a ladder set up on the earth, and the top of it reached to heaven: and behold the angels of God ascending and descending on it.Genesis 28:12

Heaven, earth and hell

In the image of man (spirit, soul, body), creation unfolds as heaven, earth and hell — a hierarchy of being. Hell is not a place God invented in order to punish: it is the state of a creature that shuts itself in refusal and moves away from the source of life. Earth is the place of encounter and choice; heaven, life wholly turned towards God. The great trap of the earth is illusion: taking the material for more “obvious” than the spiritual. To grow in being is to become “heaven” oneself, by grace.

Key 3

Prayer and the Jesus Prayer

To pray is to speak with God. Prayer as regular nourishment, and prayer as breathing that accompanies the whole of life.

To pray is to speak with God. The desire to pray arises when we have something to say to him. The danger is to mistake for prayer an inner monologue in which we talk to ourselves, placing God far away as an abstract idea: we then pray “about God” instead of praying to God.

True prayer puts us back in our rightful place: we are not much, and God is everything — and from this realisation springs not sadness but praise. Prayer is the spirit feeding on God and breathing him; little by little the spirit nourishes the soul, the soul nourishes the body, and the body itself becomes prayer.

Two forms of prayer

Prayer as foodA regular rule, like meals at set times: morning and evening prayers, psalms, reading the Gospel. A faithful quarter of an hour is better than great bursts of fervour that lead nowhere.
Prayer as breathingA continual, discreet prayer accompanying the day like our breath: “Pray without ceasing” (1 Thessalonians 5:17).

The Jesus Prayer

At the heart of the Orthodox tradition lies a very short prayer, repeated with attention and humility, often to the rhythm of one’s breathing, sometimes with the help of a woollen prayer rope (chotki in Russian, komboskini in Greek):

Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me, a sinner.The Jesus Prayer

It contains the whole Gospel: the confession of Christ as Lord and Son of God, and the cry for his mercy. The Fathers advise saying it without haste, without seeking extraordinary experiences, and being guided by a spiritual father for a more intense practice.

Practical advice for praying at home

  • Set up a prayer corner (the “beautiful corner”), facing east if possible, with one or more icons and a vigil lamp.
  • Begin with the sign of the cross and a simple prayer: “O Heavenly King”, the Trisagion, the Lord’s Prayer.
  • Set yourself a modest, sustainable rule, agreed with your priest; regularity matters more than length.
  • Turn the gestures of daily life into prayer: “Are you a craftsman? Sing psalms, even inwardly: you will make your workshop a monastery” (St John Chrysostom).
When we persevere patiently in prayer, we notice that our moods and our capricious “selves” gradually lose their authority. Prayer places man in awareness of what is real.Bishop Jean of Saint-Denis
Key 4

The Holy Icons

The icon is “the image that no longer lies”: a window onto the Kingdom, founded on the Incarnation of Christ.

In a world wounded by sin, images often deceive us: they feed illusion and bind us ever more to appearances. The icon, on the contrary, is the image that no longer lies: it nourishes prayer and draws us towards more life and light.

Why can God be depicted?

God, who is invisible, cannot be drawn. But in Jesus Christ the Invisible has become visible: the Word became flesh. Because Christ has a true human face, he can be painted. The icon therefore proclaims the Incarnation: the Godhead has united itself, without confusion, to our nature, and the whole material creation — wood, colours, gold — is called to be transfigured.

An icon is a likeness, a pattern and an image of something, showing in itself what it depicts. It reveals and shows what is hidden.St John of Damascus (8th century)

Venerating is not worshipping

The Seventh Ecumenical Council (Nicaea II, 787) clarified the distinction: worship is due to God alone; icons receive respectful veneration — a kiss, incense, light — which “passes to the prototype”, that is, to the person depicted. We do not venerate painted wood, but Christ, the Mother of God or the saint whom it makes present. The victory over iconoclasm is celebrated each year on the first Sunday of Great Lent: the Triumph of Orthodoxy.

How to “read” an icon

  • The gold background: the uncreated light of the Kingdom, with no shadow and no outside source.
  • Reverse perspective: the lines open out towards us; it is not we who look at the icon, but the icon that looks at us.
  • Peaceful faces, large eyes, closed mouths: bodies already transfigured, gathered into the silence of God.
  • An icon is “written” in prayer and fasting, according to handed-down canons, and then blessed.

On entering a church, we cross ourselves, kiss the icon of Christ and that of the Mother of God, then light a candle: its flame is an image of grace, and the burning wax signifies our life offered up.

Key 5

The Sacraments, or Mysteries

Baptism, Chrismation, Eucharist, Confession, Marriage, Ordination, Anointing of the sick: the Holy Spirit sanctifies the whole of life.

The Christian East readily speaks of Mysteries rather than sacraments: these acts through which God communicates his life to us cannot be reduced to formulas. They are not “drawers” to be opened now and then: the whole Christian life is bathed in grace, and the Church also blesses the home, fruit, water, journeys, the birth of a child…

Matter itself takes part in salvation: the water of baptism, the fragrant oil of holy chrism, the bread and wine of the Eucharist, the oil of anointing. And in every Mystery it is the Holy Spirit who acts: the whole Church is an epiclesis, an invocation of the Spirit.

BaptismThreefold immersion in the name of the Trinity: we die and rise with Christ, we “put on Christ”. Once only.
ChrismationAnointing with holy chrism, “the seal of the gift of the Holy Spirit”. The newly baptised becomes a member of the royal and priestly people.
EucharistThe Mystery of Mysteries: communion in the Body and Blood of Christ, real presence, summit of the whole life of the Church.
ConfessionTherapy of the soul: confessing one’s sins before Christ, in the presence of the priest who gives absolution. To be practised regularly.
MarriageMan and woman are crowned and united in the Spirit, as Christ is united to his Church.
OrdinationThrough the laying-on of the bishop’s hands, deacons, priests and bishops are given for the service of God’s people.
Anointing of the sickPrayer and anointing with oil for the healing of soul and body, according to God’s will.

Christian initiation

In the Orthodox Church, baptism, chrismation and first communion are given in a single celebration, including to infants when an Orthodox family undertakes to raise them in the faith. Little children thus receive communion from their baptism onwards.

The epiclesis

At the Liturgy, after recalling Christ’s words (“This is my Body… This is my Blood”), the priest calls down the Holy Spirit upon the Gifts. It is by the power of the Spirit that the bread and wine become the Body and Blood of Christ. A mystery of faith, received rather than explained.

Blessings in the home

An Orthodox family keeps holy water brought home from the great blessing of the waters at Theophany: it is drunk on an empty stomach and sprinkled around the house. Parents bless their children and the meal: the whole of creation is thus drawn into prayer.

Key 6

The Church and living Tradition

Mother of the living, unity in diversity, salt of the earth and light of the world: the Church in the image of the Trinity.

The Church cannot be reduced to a visible institution, sometimes influential, sometimes persecuted. Just as the Word who contains all things became man in history, the Church that embraces the universe has become a concrete community, founded by Christ, enlightened by the Spirit, organised by the Apostles.

Mother of the living

The Fathers see in the blood and water flowing from the side of Christ on the cross the birth of the Church, the new Eve: the blood of the Eucharist and the water of baptism. At Pentecost she receives the breath of the Spirit. She carries humanity as a mother carries her child, and nourishes it through the sacraments. Where there is the Eucharist, a baptised people and a bishop, there is the Church.

The mystery of the Church is that of Christ himself: she is divine-human (theandric), God and us, inseparably.

Unity in diversity

Because she is in the image of the Trinity — one God in three Persons — the Orthodox Church is not a centralised, uniform organisation, but a communion of local Churches: in Antioch, Athens, Moscow, Bucharest, Tokyo or Paris, each with its own language and culture, and each is the whole Church, because it celebrates the same Eucharist in the same faith. All bishops are equal in the episcopate; together they are successors of all the Apostles.

Decisions are made in agreement, in council: “It seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us…” (Acts 15:28). This is called conciliarity (in Russian, sobornost). Authority does not fall from above like a sword: it circulates throughout the baptised people like blood in a living body.

Mix faded colours and you get a dirty grey; bring together an intensely blue blue, a brilliant yellow, a passionately red red, and you get white light.An image from the catechesis: each person’s full personality makes for living unity

Living Tradition

Tradition is not the dust of the past: it is the Holy Spirit becoming the memory of the Church. It includes Holy Scripture, the Creed, the dogmas of the councils, the Liturgy, the writings of the Fathers, icons and the lives of the saints. “Truth renews the vessel that contains it,” said St Irenaeus: it is not for the Church to chase after fashions; it is the Truth that keeps her ever new. A river cut off from its source becomes a pond.

Salt of the earth, light of the world

“Ye are the salt of the earth… Ye are the light of the world” (Matthew 5:13-16). Light gives understanding: it shows each reality as it is. Salt gives life its savour: it enables us to choose life and joy. The Church is called to give meaning and savour to everything that happens in the world.

Key 7

The Divine Liturgy and the liturgical year

Entering the church, understanding the Liturgy, living by the rhythm of the feasts: from the Nativity to Pascha, the “feast of feasts”.

Entering the church

The majesty of God is perceived in the measure of our inner silence. Before entering, let us leave our grievances, worries and agitation at the door. The church is oriented: in the east is the sanctuary, an image of heaven and of the Kingdom, separated from the nave by the iconostasis. Its Holy (Royal) Doors, in the centre, are flanked by the icon of Christ (on the right) and that of the Mother of God (on the left). To the north of the sanctuary is the prothesis, where the Gifts are prepared; to the south, the diakonikon.

On arriving, we take a candle, cross ourselves, venerate the icons, and may hand the priest a list of the names of the living and the departed for whom we would like him to pray.

“Wisdom! Stand aright!” — Attentive, in silence

We stand before God: the whole congregation celebrates together with the angels and the clergy (seats are of course available for the elderly or tired). Let us attend: become a cup ready to receive the treasures of the Liturgy. In silence: not only without chatting, but by quieting our inner chatter. What matters is what God is going to say to us.

The two parts of the Divine Liturgy

  • The Liturgy of the Catechumens, devoted to teaching: the great litany, antiphons, the Little Entrance with the Gospel book, the Trisagion (“Holy God, Holy Mighty, Holy Immortal, have mercy on us”), the reading of the Epistle and Gospel, the homily, prayers for the Church and the world.
  • The Liturgy of the Faithful, devoted to the Eucharist: the Cherubic Hymn and the Great Entrance with the Gifts, the kiss of peace, the Creed sung by all, the anaphora and epiclesis, the Lord’s Prayer, communion. These mysteries are lived more than described: “one must take part”.
Let us who mystically represent the Cherubim, and who sing the thrice-holy hymn to the life-creating Trinity, now lay aside all earthly cares.Cherubic Hymn

The liturgical year

The Byzantine liturgical year begins on 1 September and culminates at Pascha, the “feast of feasts”. Around it are arranged the twelve great feasts. Our parish, like the Russian Church, follows the Julian calendar: fixed feasts fall 13 days later than in the civil calendar.

Great Lent and Holy Week

Prepared for by the pre-Lenten Sundays (the Publican and the Pharisee, the Prodigal Son, the Last Judgement), Great Lent opens with Forgiveness Vespers, at which everyone asks forgiveness of everyone. The first Sunday celebrates the Triumph of Orthodoxy (the restoration of icons), the second St Gregory Palamas, theologian of the divine light. Then come Holy Week — Great Thursday of the Last Supper, Great Friday of the Cross, Great Saturday — and the night of Pascha: “Christ is risen! — Truly he is risen!”

The Creed in questions

Practical questions

From the Greek orthos, “straight, right”, and doxa, which means both “opinion” and “glory”. Orthodoxy is therefore right belief and right glorification of God. The two are inseparable: we believe as we pray, and we pray as we believe.

The Orthodox Church understands herself as the “one, holy, catholic and apostolic Church” of the Creed, in continuity with the Church of the Apostles and the Fathers. For a Western Christian, discovering Orthodoxy often means rediscovering the common faith of the first millennium — that of St Irenaeus of Lyons, St Martin of Tours or St Germanus of Paris.

It is not an equation to solve but a revealed mystery: God is one nature in three Persons. The Father, the Son and the Spirit are not three gods, nor three “masks” of one God: each is fully God, and they are one in love.

This mystery sheds light on our own life: a person truly exists only in relationship, in self-giving to others. The Church, a communion of unique persons, is called to be the icon of the Trinity.

Because our salvation depends on it. If Christ were not truly God, he could not unite us to God. If he were not truly man, our humanity would not have been reached and healed. The Fathers put it this way: “What has not been assumed has not been healed” (St Gregory of Nazianzus).

The Council of Chalcedon (451) formulated it: one Person in two natures, “without confusion or separation”. This balance protects the Christian life from two distortions: despising the human in the name of God, or forgetting God in the name of the human.

It is the goal of the Christian life: to become, by grace, a partaker of the divine life (2 Peter 1:4). Not to dissolve into God or lose one’s personality, but to be transfigured by his light, as iron plunged into fire becomes burning hot without ceasing to be iron.

This path passes through the sacraments, prayer, the commandments and love of neighbour. God’s grace and our freedom work together in it: this is synergy.

The Orthodox Church keeps the text of the Creed as the Ecumenical Councils of Nicaea and Constantinople established it, in keeping with Christ’s words: “the Spirit of truth, which proceedeth from the Father” (John 15:26). The Western addition of the Filioque (“and the Son”) came later, without an ecumenical council.

For the Orthodox tradition this is not a quarrel over words: affirming the Father as the sole source of the Son and of the Spirit preserves the equal dignity of the three Persons, and is reflected even in the conciliar life of the Church.

No. An idol claims to enclose the divine in an object; the icon, on the other hand, bears witness that God has become visible in Jesus Christ. Because the Son of God took on a human face, he can be depicted.

The Second Council of Nicaea (787) distinguishes worship, due to God alone, from the veneration given to icons. As St Basil says, “the honour paid to the image passes to the prototype”: in kissing an icon we greet the person it represents, just as we might kiss the photograph of a loved one.

In Christ, death does not separate: the saints are alive in God and form one Church with us. Asking them to pray for us is like asking a friend or relative to pray for us — with the difference that they are already in the light of God.

The Mother of God holds a unique place: she said “yes” on behalf of all humanity, and Christ on the cross gave her to us as our Mother. Orthodox Christians pray to her with great tenderness: “Most Holy Theotokos, save us!”

  • A place: an icon corner, facing east if possible, with a vigil lamp.
  • A time: morning and evening, even briefly. Regularity nourishes the soul like meals taken at set times.
  • Words: those of the Church — “O Heavenly King”, the Trisagion, the Lord’s Prayer, a psalm, the morning and evening prayers from an Orthodox prayer book. Then your own words, simply.
  • A rule: modest and faithful, agreed with your spiritual father. “Pray for just a quarter of an hour a day, but really do it,” Bishop Jean of Saint-Denis advised.
  • Throughout the day: the Jesus Prayer, a sign of the cross before meals, a thought for those you love.

Join the first three fingers of the right hand (thumb, index and middle finger) as a sign of the Trinity, and fold the other two against the palm as a sign of the two natures of Christ. Touch the forehead, the chest, then the right shoulder and finally the left — that is, from right to left, unlike the Latin practice.

We cross ourselves on entering the church, before icons, at invocations of the Trinity during services, and before and after meals. It is often accompanied by a deep bow or a prostration (metania).

You are very welcome! No one will expect you to know everything. A few pointers:

  • Modest dress; by tradition, women often cover their heads and men uncover theirs.
  • At the entrance you may take a candle and place it before an icon.
  • During services we stand; do not hesitate to sit down if you need to.
  • Watch, listen, let yourself be carried by the singing: the Liturgy is understood first of all by living it.
  • At the end, everyone comes to venerate the cross; non-Orthodox may receive the blessed bread (antidoron) given out at that moment.

After the service, introduce yourself to the priest: he will be glad to welcome you and answer your questions.

The Liturgy is not a show to watch: the whole congregation celebrates together. Every “Amen”, every “Kyrie eleison” (“Lord, have mercy”) is our participation. We sing the Creed and the Lord’s Prayer with the choir, bow at the blessings and cross ourselves at the invocations of the Trinity.

Arriving before the start allows us to recollect ourselves and to hand in our prayer intentions. The deacon’s call sums up the inner attitude: “Wisdom! Stand aright! Let us attend!”

Standing is the posture of the risen one and of the attentive servant: we stand before God as the angels stand before his throne. It is also the posture of vigilance — a person standing is a person awake.

But the Church is a mother: seats are provided for the elderly, the sick and the tired, and one may sit during some readings or the homily. Better to sit thinking of God than to stand thinking of one’s feet!

Communion in the Body and Blood of Christ is reserved for baptised and chrismated Orthodox faithful. It is the heart of the Christian life, and we should receive it often — but never out of routine. The usual preparation includes:

  • Peace with everyone: being reconciled with those with whom we are at odds (Matthew 5:23-24).
  • Prayer: on the eve, attending Vespers or the Vigil if possible, and reading the prayers before Holy Communion.
  • The eucharistic fast: eating and drinking nothing from midnight (or from waking) until communion, unless medically necessary.
  • Confession, as often as agreed with one’s spiritual father.
  • Arriving on time: being present from the beginning of the Liturgy, and especially for the reading of the Gospel.

At communion, we approach with arms crossed over the chest, say our baptismal name, receive the Gifts from the spoon, then take a little warm water and blessed bread. Each parish has its customs: talk to the priest about it.

I will not speak of thy mystery to thine enemies, neither like Judas will I give thee a kiss; but like the thief will I confess thee: Remember me, O Lord, in thy Kingdom.Prayer before communion

Confession (the Mystery of Repentance) is a therapy of the soul, to which we turn regularly throughout life, already during the catechumenate. It is not a tribunal but a hospital: we come to it as a sick person who wants to be healed.

In the Orthodox tradition, one usually confesses standing before an icon of Christ and the Gospel book, with the priest beside us as a witness: it is to Christ that we confess. The priest may give advice, then lays his stole (epitrachelion) on the penitent’s head and reads the prayer of absolution.

To prepare: examine your conscience in the light of the commandments and the Beatitudes, name your faults simply without justifying yourself or getting lost in details, and sincerely desire to change — the meaning of the Greek word metanoia, “turning around”.

He who knows his own sin is greater than he who raises the dead.St Isaac the Syrian

Fasting is neither a diet nor a punishment: it is a way of putting the body back in its proper place, of freeing time and attention for prayer, and of sharing with the poor. It prepares the joy of the feasts: we fast in order to rejoice the more.

The Church proposes four great fasts (Great Lent before Pascha, the Nativity fast, the Apostles’ fast and the Dormition fast), as well as Wednesdays and Fridays. Traditionally one then abstains from meat, dairy products and eggs, and sometimes from fish, wine and oil.

Fasting is practised with discernment and without pride, according to one’s health and circumstances, and in agreement with one’s spiritual father. “What good is it to abstain from meat if you devour your brother by slander?” (St Basil).

One does not “sign up” for the Church: one is received at the end of a journey. The first step is to come to the services and meet the priest. Then begins the catechumenate: a time of teaching, prayer and participation in the life of the community, whose length varies from person to person.

Entry into the Church is through baptism, chrismation and communion. For someone already baptised in another Christian confession, the Church determines the manner of reception case by case — most often by chrismation, preceded by a profession of faith.

As Fr Georges Lusseaud’s catechesis puts it: you do not board a train without knowing where it is going. Take the time to get to know the faith: that is precisely the purpose of these pages.

The godparent is the guarantor of the faith of the newly baptised before the Church. For a child, the godparent renounces Satan and unites to Christ in the child’s name, then recites the Creed. He or she undertakes to accompany the child through life by prayer and example.

That is why a godparent must be a practising Orthodox Christian. One is enough (preferably of the same sex as the person baptised); custom often provides for two.

The Russian Church, like several other Orthodox Churches (Jerusalem, Serbia, Georgia, Mount Athos…), follows the Julian calendar for its feasts. Christmas is indeed celebrated on 25 December, but that day currently corresponds to 7 January in the civil (Gregorian) calendar, a difference of 13 days.

The date of Pascha, for its part, is calculated by all Orthodox Churches according to the rule of the First Council of Nicaea: it sometimes coincides with Western Easter, and often differs from it by one to five weeks.

The Orthodox Church has always celebrated in the language of the peoples it evangelised: Saints Cyril and Methodius thus created liturgical Slavonic for the Slavs. In Russian parishes, services are celebrated in Slavonic, often with parts in French, the language of the country where our parish lives.

Do not let language hold you back: bilingual booklets exist, and you can ask the priest for the texts of the Liturgy in English or French. Gestures, icons and singing also speak to the heart.

Because love is stronger than death. In Christ, the living and the departed form one Church; our prayers accompany those who have left us on their way to God. The Church celebrates services for them (panikhidas), especially on the 3rd, 9th and 40th days after death, and on the “Saturdays of the departed”.

At each Liturgy the priest commemorates the names of the living and the departed that the faithful entrust to him: do not hesitate to hand them to him when you arrive.

No one walks the spiritual path alone. Most often, the priest to whom we confess becomes our spiritual father: he helps us to discern, to establish a suitable rule of prayer and fasting, and to avoid discouragement as well as exaltation.

He is not a director who decides for us, but an experienced companion who helps us become free before God.
