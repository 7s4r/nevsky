Your first visit to an Orthodox church

01 You are welcome

An Orthodox church is first of all a place of prayer. People come to pray, but also to discover, to listen and to look. You can simply stand at the back and watch: nobody will ask where you come from or what you believe.

The church is currently closed for renovation. Before you come, check the service calendar on the home page, subscribe to the newsletter or contact the parish.

02 Before you come

  • Service times. Announced services are shown in the parish calendar. On Sunday morning the Divine Liturgy is the main service; the Vigil is celebrated the evening before.
  • What to wear. Modest clothing is enough. By tradition, in Russian churches women often cover their heads with a scarf and wear a skirt, and men remove their hats. On a first visit nobody will reproach you.
  • Arriving a little early lets you light a candle and find your place quietly. If you arrive late, simply come in quietly: it happens often.
  • Your phone: please switch it to silent. Always ask permission before taking photos.

03 On entering

On entering, many of the faithful make the sign of the cross and bow. You are not obliged to, but you may if you wish.

  • Candles. You may take a candle (an offering is customary) and place it before an icon, praying for yourself, for your loved ones or for the departed.
  • Icons. The faithful venerate them by crossing themselves and kissing them. Icons are not decorations: they are “windows” onto Christ, the Mother of God and the saints.
  • The iconostasis is the wall of icons separating the nave from the sanctuary. Only the clergy and servers enter the sanctuary.

04 During the service

  • People stand, as before a king, as a sign of respect and of the resurrection. If you need to, sit down without hesitation: there are always a few seats.
  • Everything is sung, by the choir, without instruments. The Liturgy lasts about an hour and a half.
  • The faithful move about: they cross themselves, bow, go to light a candle. Do as you feel; what matters is being present.
  • Children are welcome and part of the assembly. If they get restless, you can step out for a moment and come back.
We knew not whether we were in heaven or on earth.The envoys of Prince Vladimir, after a Liturgy in Constantinople (Primary Chronicle)

Let yourself be carried by the singing, the incense and the light: the Liturgy is understood first of all by living it. The catechism explains its main parts.

05 Communion and the end of the service

Holy Communion is the heart of the Liturgy. It is received by Orthodox faithful who have prepared through prayer, fasting and usually confession. This is not a judgement on others: communion expresses the unity of one faith.

At the end of the service, everyone comes forward to venerate the cross held by the priest. Non-Orthodox visitors may receive the blessed bread (antidoron) given out at that moment.

06 And afterwards?

After the service, introduce yourself to the priest or to a parishioner: they will be glad to welcome you and answer your questions. To go further, the catechism and the prayer book are available on this site.

In short

  • Modest clothing; a headscarf for women if you wish.
  • A candle before an icon, if you like.
  • Standing during the service, but sit down if needed.
  • Communion is for the Orthodox; the blessed bread is offered to all.
  • Come and talk to the priest after the service.
Frequently asked questions

Your first visit in questions

No. Anyone can come to pray or simply attend a service. Many visitors come out of curiosity, with friends, or in search of God.

In Russian parishes, services are celebrated in Church Slavonic, often with parts in the local language. Ask the parish; the prayer book on this site gives the main prayers in English and Slavonic.

The Sunday Divine Liturgy lasts about an hour and a half; the Saturday evening Vigil a little longer. You can come for part of it.

Talk to the priest. One enters the Church through Baptism and Chrismation, after a time of preparation (the catechumenate). See also How do I become Orthodox?
